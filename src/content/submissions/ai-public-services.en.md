A look back, written in October 2026, at the plan to bring AI into government
work, first set out in the 2025 Policy Address.

#### What changed

**A team and a target.** The 2025 Policy Address (17 September 2025) set up an
AI Efficacy Enhancement Team, led by the Deputy Chief Secretary for
Administration, to bring AI into the government's day-to-day work. The target is
AI in **100 administrative procedures during 2026**, and **at least 200 by the
end of 2027**.

**The first 30 projects.** On **17 August 2026**, the government listed the
first batch of 30 projects across 13 departments. They cover work such as
transport licences, sports facility bookings, food licences, environmental
hygiene, complaint handling and environmental protection.

#### Who it reaches

Almost everyone who deals with the government: anyone applying for a licence,
booking a sports facility or making a complaint, and the officers who handle
those cases.

#### What's happened since

- The government says that in some of the first projects, the time needed to
  **triage or analyse a case has been cut by half or more**, and that waiting
  times for some cases have fallen by up to 90%.
- According to the South China Morning Post, AI has roughly halved the time
  the Environmental Protection Department takes to review environmental impact
  assessments.

#### Where AI should decide, and where it should only flag

The targets count procedures. They don't say what the AI is allowed to do in
each one. We'd sort every use into one of three groups:

1. **AI does the task.** Routine steps where a mistake is easy to spot and
   undo: checking an application has every document, offering booking slots,
   answering questions from published rules.
2. **AI flags, an officer decides.** Anything that affects someone's money,
   rights or safety: sorting complaints, deciding which inspections come first,
   refusing a licence. The AI can sort and suggest, but a named officer makes
   the call, and the person can ask why.
3. **AI stays out.** Decisions that depend on someone's circumstances, where an
   error is hard to see and hard to put right, such as hardship appeals and
   exceptions to the rules.

**Triage is where the line matters most.** Cutting triage time in half is a
real gain. But if AI decides which cases get looked at first, a case it sorts
wrongly can sit unread, and nobody complains about a delay they don't know
about. Triage belongs in the second group.

#### The case for

**Faster service.** Licences, bookings and replies that took days can take
hours.

**Officers' time goes where it's needed.** Less time on routine sorting means
more on the hard cases.

**A public target.** 100 procedures in 2026 and 200 by 2027 is something the
public can check.

#### The case against

**Counting procedures isn't counting quality.** The targets measure how many
procedures use AI, not how often it gets things wrong.

**Triage mistakes are invisible.** A case pushed down the queue doesn't show up
as an error unless someone goes looking for it.

**The public can't see the line.** Nothing published yet says which projects
let AI decide and which only let it flag.

#### Our recommendation

1. **Publish, for every project, which group it's in** and who is responsible
   for its decisions.
2. **For triage, report how often officers overrule the AI**, and check a sample
   of low-priority cases by hand.
3. **Tell people when AI was used on their case**, and how to ask for a person
   to look at it again.

#### What we're watching

- Whether 100 procedures are reached by the end of 2026.
- Whether the government publishes error and overrule rates alongside time
  saved.
- What the next batch of projects lets AI do.
