Written with the 2025 Policy Address commitments on digital education and the
Education Bureau's AI for Empowering Learning and Teaching Funding Programme in
mind, ahead of the 2026–27 Budget.

#### The issue

**The money is moving faster than the method.** The 2025 Policy Address
committed to an AI literacy learning framework, AI in the core curriculum and
more AI training for teachers, backed by HK$2 billion from the Quality Education
Fund. On 16 December 2025, the Education Bureau launched the AI for Empowering
Learning and Teaching Funding Programme, giving each public sector and Direct
Subsidy Scheme school HK$500,000 to use between 2025/26 and 2027/28. That's a
serious commitment. But nothing yet tells a primary teacher *how* a
seven-year-old should use AI, or when they shouldn't.

**How AI is used matters more than whether it's used.** The strongest evidence
so far is a randomised trial of nearly a thousand high-school maths students,
published in *PNAS* in June 2025 (Bastani et al.). Students given a standard
ChatGPT-style assistant scored 48% higher during practice, but when the AI was
taken away for the exam they scored 17% *lower* than students who never had it.
A second group used an AI tutor that gave hints instead of answers, and that
design largely removed the harm. If teenagers can be set back like this, six- to
twelve-year-olds, who are still building the basic skills AI most easily
replaces, are at greater risk. UNESCO's 2023 guidance recommends 13 as the
minimum age for using generative AI independently.

**Two gaps fall on the same children.**

- *Access.* Legislative Council research found that in 2019, 94% of Hong Kong
  households had internet access, but only 71% of households earning under
  HK$10,000 a month did, and up to 13% of primary pupils had no computer or
  tablet of their own.
- *Guidance.* A child from a better-off family usually has a parent or tutor
  who can show them how to use AI well: ask for a hint, check the answer,
  explain it back. A grassroots child is more likely to have no AI at all, or
  AI with nobody guiding them.

A flat HK$500,000 per school closes neither gap. A school where most families
pass the means test gets exactly the same as a school where few do.

**Where this comes from.** This proposal grew out of our own tutoring. The
children we teach have never been short of interest. What they lack is access,
and someone to show them how to use these tools in a way that builds their
thinking instead of replacing it.

#### What we propose

**1. An age-staged "Think First" ladder in the AI literacy framework**

| Stage | Principle | In class |
|---|---|---|
| P1–P3 | Think without AI | No individual pupil accounts on generative AI. Teachers can show AI to the whole class, always framed as "Is the computer right?" Lessons build the habits AI can't supply: explaining how you know, estimating before calculating, finding the mistake in a worked example. |
| P4–P6 | Think, then check with AI | Supervised use only, in "hint mode", following the four steps below. |
| S1–S3 | Think with AI | Guided independent use, with assessment that rewards the student's own reasoning, not just the final answer. |

**2. A four-step protocol for every AI-assisted primary lesson**

1. **Attempt.** The child works on the problem alone first, for at least the
   first third of the time.
2. **Ask.** The child asks the AI for a hint, a question or a check, never for
   the answer.
3. **Check.** The child compares the AI's reply with their own attempt and
   finds at least one difference, or explains why the two agree.
4. **Explain.** With the AI off the screen, the child explains the answer in
   their own words.

The last step is the safeguard. If a child can explain the answer without the
AI, they learned it. If they can't, the AI did the thinking.

**3. "Hint mode" as a condition on public money.** Any AI tool bought for
primary pupils with public funds, including the HK$500,000 grant, should
default to hints and guiding questions rather than full answers, let teachers
see pupils' prompts and the AI's replies, and never use children's data to
train AI models.

**4. Think First Labs: a three-year pilot in 100 primary schools.** The 100
public primary schools with the highest share of pupils on full-grant School
Textbook Assistance, a means-tested measure the government already collects,
would run two 90-minute after-school sessions a week for P4–P6 pupils. One
teacher, supported by trained secondary-school and university tutors at about
one tutor to six pupils, would use school-owned devices on site, so no child
needs a device or broadband at home to take part.

**5. Teacher training.** A six-hour "Teaching thinking with AI" module through
the Education Bureau's existing professional development programme, with at
least two teachers trained in every public primary school by the end of the
2026/27 school year.

#### Cost

| Per school, per year | HK$ |
|---|---|
| Teacher and tutor stipends | 130,000 |
| Devices, connectivity and materials | 35,000 |
| Independent evaluation | 15,000 |
| **Total** | **180,000** |

100 schools × HK$180,000 × 3 years = **HK$54 million**, about 2.7% of the HK$2
billion already set aside in the Quality Education Fund for digital education.
The training module and the hint-mode condition fit within existing budgets.

#### The case for

- **It protects learning.** The best evidence we have shows that AI built to
  give hints instead of answers avoids the learning loss that unrestricted AI
  causes. The protocol builds that into how lessons run, not just into the
  software.
- **It reaches the children who stand to gain most.** Better-off children
  already get guided AI use at home. The Labs give grassroots children the
  same, at a fraction of the cost of private tutoring.
- **It uses data the government already has.** School Textbook Assistance
  records identify the schools in most need without a new means test.
- **It's cheap and testable.** At under 3% of the existing allocation, it can
  be evaluated properly before anyone scales it up.

#### The case against

- **It holds younger children back from AI.** Keeping individual accounts out
  of P1–P3 means younger children miss some benefits, and some parents and
  schools will feel Hong Kong is falling behind more permissive systems.
- **It takes class time.** Trying first and explaining afterwards makes AI
  lessons slower in an already crowded curriculum.
- **Hint mode can be got around.** A determined child can rephrase a question
  until the AI gives the answer, and a buying condition can't fully stop that.
- **The evidence for young children is thin.** The main trial was with
  high-school students. Effects on primary pupils could differ either way.
- **The money might do more elsewhere.** HK$54 million could pay for smaller
  classes or more teaching assistants, which have a longer track record.

#### Our recommendation

On balance, we recommend all five measures. The case against is strongest on
the evidence, which is exactly why the Labs should start as an independently
evaluated three-year pilot rather than a territory-wide rollout. The cost of
getting this wrong, a generation who can use AI but can't think without it, is
far higher than the cost of moving carefully.

#### How we'd measure it

- **The key test.** After each AI-assisted unit, pupils sit a short test
  without AI. Lab pupils should match or beat similar pupils who learned the
  same unit without AI. If they score lower, AI is replacing their thinking and
  the approach has to change.
- **Reasoning.** Teachers score each pupil's "Explain" step against a simple
  four-level rubric.
- **Reach.** At least 70% attendance by eligible P4–P6 pupils in Lab schools.
- **Results.** Internal assessment results and class banding, compared with
  similar schools outside the pilot.

#### What's happened since

In June 2026, the Education Bureau published its Blueprint for Digital
Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools. It includes an AI
Literacy Learning Framework and a Guide to Using AI in Teaching, and requires
teachers to complete at least 30 hours of digital education training in every
three-year professional development cycle, starting from the 2026/27 school
year.
